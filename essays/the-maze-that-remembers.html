The Maze That Remembers

How AI search scales when failures become shared knowledge.

Dana Edwards · October 7, 2026 · With an interactive model and Lean proofs

The math releaseTry the mazeThe scaling argumentScientific memoryInspect the proof

I think of an AI research system as a group of agents exploring a maze. Some paths lead somewhere useful. Most do not. The important question is what the next agent knows about the paths already explored.

Without shared memory, every agent can rediscover the same dead end. With a reliable record, a failed attempt becomes information the whole system can use. More compute can buy more exploration, while better memory reduces how much exploration is wasted.

This is part of why I built Research Kernel MCP and PopperPad. I want a research process that retains its mistakes, preserves the evidence, and gives the next investigation a better starting point.

My claim is conditional but strong: when the search space is finite and correctly specified, checks finish, rejected candidates really are invalid, and work is distributed without duplication, additional workers can reduce the time needed to cover the space. We can prove that statement. Whether a real AI research system satisfies those conditions is a separate, measurable question.

What OpenAI's math release changes

The October release of openai/math makes this discussion concrete. At the revision I inspected, the repository lists 722 manuscripts in 372 result families. OpenAI reports posing approximately 4,000 problems to an unreleased internal model, with most results produced through a common procedure averaging three hours of ChatGPT Pro thinking compute per result. The collection includes work on the irrationality exponent of π and free group factors.1

The repository also says that verification is at different stages, that some results lack Lean formalizations, and that unformalized results may have issues. I have not independently replayed this collection. Manuscript counts, result families, and problems posed are different units; dividing these numbers would not establish a solve rate. A released manuscript is a research claim whose exact statement, proof, and review status still matter.

What interests me is the emerging workflow: generate mathematical candidates at scale, then spend verification and human attention on deciding what survived. The possibility is substantial even without treating every announced result as settled.

Proposal and proof checking are different jobs

In the conversation that prompted this essay, I described AI as a proof generator that relies on Lean. I would make that more precise now. A model can develop arguments, recognize errors, revise an approach, and call checking tools. Its confidence does not supply the formal guarantee.

Lean checks a proof of an exact formal statement relative to its logical foundations and declared assumptions. Someone must still inspect whether that statement expresses the intended problem. An omitted hypothesis, a weakened conclusion, or an inconsistent assumption can make an impressive-looking proof irrelevant. The axiom dependencies also matter: a proof admitted through sorry is not the evidence I want.2

Reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards, or RLVR, uses externally checkable outcomes as a training signal. Tülu 3 is one primary research example; the awesome-RLVR collection is a useful reading index.3 Training can make useful candidates more likely. It does not make every completion correct, remove sparse-reward problems, or guarantee that the reward measures what we intended.

I separate three things: the generator proposes, the checker evaluates a declared obligation, and a person decides whether that obligation captures the question worth answering. This architecture works without resolving the philosophical debate over what counts as reasoning.

The maze needs a boundary

“The search space is finite” needs an explicit qualification. All of mathematics is not one finite maze. A bounded proof search can be finite: fix a finite alphabet, a maximum proof length, a formal language, and a checking budget. There are then finitely many candidate strings. A proof may exist outside that length bound, or a true statement may have no proof in the chosen system.

Even a finite maze has infinitely many possible walks if an agent can loop forever. We need to bound path length or search a finite set of relevant states. States may be merged only when the information discarded cannot change which solutions remain possible.

The small model below uses 32 terminal routes through a branching maze, with one hidden goal. Every route has five decisions. One check evaluates a complete route; generating it, traversing it, and checking it are treated as one equal-cost unit. This is a model of candidate allocation, not a benchmark of LLM intelligence.

A finite maze, two ways to search

Both searches use the same maze and worker count. One allocates distinct routes and retains failures. The other uses seeded pseudorandom sampling with replacement, so agents can repeat work. Each batch finishes before the next begins.

Coverage guarantee: at this worker count, distinct allocation covers all 32 routes within 8 rounds. Either search may find the goal earlier. A lucky sampler can win a particular run.

The interactive comparison needs JavaScript. The result without it: 32 distinct checks take at most 32, 16, 8, or 4 rounds with 1, 2, 4, or 8 workers respectively. Random sampling can repeat indefinitely.

The difference is visible in the repeat count. The memory itself does not assign work. A coordinator must reserve candidates before dispatch, then record the result. If agents all read the same old snapshot and start the same task, a shared database alone will not stop duplication.

What more proposals buy

Suppose each proposal has a 10% chance of passing the stated checker. If that marginal rate stays fixed as volume rises, the expected number of accepted proposals is:

E[accepted proposals] = mp

Linearity of expectation does not require independence. But this counts accepted proposals, which could include the same theorem many times. It does not count distinct discoveries, valuable discoveries, or independently replicated scientific results.

For independent attempts at the same target, each with the same success probability p, the probability of at least one success after m attempts is:

P(at least one success) = 1 − (1 − p)m

At p = 0.10, 44 independent attempts give about a 99.03% chance of at least one success. If every agent makes the same attempt, adding copies can leave that probability at 10%. If the generator has zero probability of reaching the needed proof, additional samples from that unchanged generator will never find it. These are probability calculations under stated assumptions; the accompanying Lean file proves the finite scheduling and pruning results below, not this probabilistic model.

A stronger guarantee from distinct work

Now let U be a fixed finite set of N candidates. Suppose a total checker correctly decides whether each candidate satisfies the target predicate. Allocate each candidate once, with k ≥ 1 workers and one equal-cost completed check per worker per round. Full coverage takes:

Tk(N) = ⌈N / k⌉

Why? After r rounds there are at most rk check slots. Fewer than ⌈N/k⌉ rounds cannot hold all N candidates. Conversely, assign candidate i to round ⌊i/k⌋ and worker i mod k. Every candidate gets a unique slot within that bound. If a solution is in U, it is checked by then. If none is accepted, the conclusion is confined to U and requires complete, correctly scoped checks.

For k₂ ≥ k₁ ≥ 1:

Tk₂(N) ≤ Tk₁(N)

This is a guarantee about the model, and the improvement is non-strict: rounding and small workloads create plateaus. It is also a latency result. Full coverage still costs N checks. More workers supply more simultaneous compute; they do not automatically reduce total work or improve the quality of any one proposal. A finite search can still be exponentially large and impractical.

What a dead end is allowed to mean

My maze analogy uses digital trails: an agent leaves evidence that changes where later agents search. But a trail must say what actually happened. “This route failed” and “nothing beyond this junction can work” are very different claims.

RecordWhat it justifies
A rejected proof scriptThat exact artifact failed that check in that environment. Another proof of the same theorem may work.
A verified counterexampleThe precise conjecture is false under the recorded assumptions. A narrower or revised conjecture may survive.
A timeout, crash, or missing toolThe attempt is unresolved. It may inform scheduling; it cannot establish mathematical impossibility.
A proof that a branch has no solutionThe whole branch can be pruned within the exact scope of that proof.

Let R ⊆ U contain only candidates certified invalid for this exact target and revision. Then removing R preserves every valid candidate. If D = |R|, the remaining workload is N − D and the additional search takes at most ⌈(N − D)/k⌉ rounds in the same model. Producing and validating those earlier rejection records was work too; the savings come from reusing it.

A receipt should identify the claim and artifact, assumptions, checker and toolchain, input hashes, result, and replay procedure. A changed dataset, theorem statement, or environment can make an old rejection inapplicable. Content hashes help identify what was checked; they do not prove the checker was right. Negative memory needs scrutiny because a bad rejection can hide the very solution we wanted.

Why Research Kernel MCP and PopperPad exist

I want an investigation to accumulate useful state across agents and sessions. These projects make claims, checks, and failures addressable rather than leaving them buried in a chat transcript.

ProjectRole in the research loop
Research Kernel MCPAn MCP interface to persistent research records, typed relationships, evidence, refutation attempts, and a frontier of next actions. Deterministic promotion rules require the specified evidence and provenance before marking a claim supported.4
PopperPadA broader scientific-memory ledger built around immutable objects, append-only history, replay recipes, counterexamples, and a derived claim graph. It also explores incentives for useful challenges and corrections.5

They preserve the record needed for coordinated search. They do not, by storing it, establish that every rejection is sound, force every agent to retrieve it, or guarantee duplicate-free execution. Those are obligations for the scheduler, checker, and integration. The theorem in this essay is an abstract contract for that architecture, not a formal verification of either repository.

Research Kernel's supported status means its local evidence gate passed. PopperPad is a public alpha; its README identifies limits around recipe isolation and automated bounty settlement. Those implementation boundaries belong beside the vision.

In empirical science the boundary is larger. A passed experiment supports a hypothesis under a design, dataset, and uncertainty model. It does not prove a universal law. Negative findings, failed replications, and alternative explanations are therefore essential parts of the memory.

What the Lean file actually proves

Eight checked statements · Lean 4.19.0

The proof uses Lean's standard library. It contains no sorry or custom axioms. The printed dependencies are confined to propext, Classical.choice, and Quot.sound. These are elementary scheduling and filtering results; I am not claiming a new mathematical discovery.

  • Completion by round r is equivalent to N ≤ rk.
  • More workers and less work cannot increase the completion round.
  • Every candidate is assigned within the bound, and distinct candidates cannot share a slot.
  • Sound pruning preserves every solution; combining pruning with more workers preserves the bound.

Read the complete Lean source · Replay instructions and verification record

Exact combined theorem statement
theorem memory_and_workers {α : Type} (candidates : List α)
    (good : α → Prop) (rejected : α → Bool) (hs : SoundRejections good rejected)
    (k₁ k₂ : Nat) (hk₁ : 0 < k₁) (h : k₁ ≤ k₂) :
    (∀ x, (x ∈ remaining candidates rejected ∧ good x) ↔
      (x ∈ candidates ∧ good x)) ∧
    rounds (remaining candidates rejected).length k₂ ≤ rounds candidates.length k₁

Here SoundRejections good rejected means ∀x, rejected(x) = true → ¬good(x). remaining filters rejected entries out of the finite list. To interpret list positions as distinct candidates, the original enumeration must itself have no duplicates.

This proves a reusable relationship between assumptions and conclusions. It does not prove that adding agents to a particular model improves real-world performance, that the goal exists, that a search bound contains it, or that an experiment settles a scientific question.

Efficiency and the possibility of a feedback loop

The next possibility is that agents help find algorithms or architectures that make further search cheaper. If the cost per useful checked candidate falls, a fixed budget can explore more candidates. If some of those candidates produce further verified efficiency gains, the process can feed back into itself.

That is a plausible route toward recursive improvement. Its continuation is an empirical hypothesis. The gains could plateau, fail to transfer to useful workloads, or move the bottleneck into memory bandwidth, verification, communication, or experiments.

The linked Codebook-Quantization prototype illustrates why measurement matters. Its README reports a Qwen3-1.7B configuration using less VRAM while running at 11.3 tokens/second versus 26.3 uncompressed. A different configuration reports a speed gain while also introducing an optimized kernel. Those are author-reported, setup-specific observations, not my independent benchmark.6 Lossless weight representation alone does not guarantee faster inference, identical floating-point execution, or lower cost per verified discovery.

A useful operational ceiling is:

completed checks / time ≤ min(kg, v)

Here g is each worker's candidate-generation rate and v is aggregate checker capacity. Communication, setup, unequal jobs, and dependency chains can reduce throughput further. More agents help only while there is useful parallel work and capacity to finish evaluating it.

I would measure distinct checked candidates per dollar, repeated attempts, verifier backlog, time to a useful result, and failures caused by stale or overbroad memory. Those measures would tell me whether the maze is being explored faster.

The implication I take seriously

The conversation began with a warning about cryptographic preparedness. More capable mathematical search is a reason to examine assumptions and the ability to replace cryptographic components. The sources here do not demonstrate an attack on a deployed cryptosystem or establish a date when one will break. A finite key space can remain computationally prohibitive. A warning and a working attack are different kinds of evidence.

My broader expectation is that much of the gain will come from systems that combine proposal quality, parallel search, reliable checking, and cumulative memory. Better models improve where we look. More compute increases how much we can try. Evidence tells us what to keep, revise, or reject.

I want to build research systems in which an honest failure has continuing value. The next agent should be able to inspect it, understand its scope, and spend its effort somewhere more useful.

Better choices can make the record more valuable

My follow-up, Research Taste and the Value of Memory, examines TasteVal and the possibility that agents will become increasingly capable experimental planners. That adds a second lever to the maze: improving the choice of routes as well as the rate of exploration.

If shared memory recovers usable compute after its overhead, better experimental judgment can increase the benefit of that recovered capacity. The follow-up makes the assumptions explicit and proposes a direct test of Research Kernel MCP with both human and AI coordination in view.
