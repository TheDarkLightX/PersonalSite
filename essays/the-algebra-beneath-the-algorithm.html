The Algebra Beneath the Algorithm

The laws that let one algorithm work in many domains.

Dana Edwards · November 12, 2026 · Research essay

Explore sections

An algorithms textbook can feel like a collection of unrelated tricks. Fast exponentiation gets one explanation; graph algorithms get another. But strip away the concrete data types, and many procedures depend on the same small set of laws. A new application can sometimes reuse an existing procedure with different values and operations.

If a procedure needs an associative operation, matrix multiplication may serve it just as well as integer addition. The useful question is:

What must remain true for this procedure to work?

Alexander Stepanov and Paul McJones build Elements of Programming around the relationship between algorithms and their mathematical requirements. Finding the weakest requirements tells us how far we can reuse a procedure.1

Four connections are a useful starting point, though they vary in strength. Reusing a loop is a closer relationship than reusing a proof technique.

Change the grouping, keep the answer

Suppose we combine four values. If the operation is associative, we can move the parentheses without changing their order:

((a ⊗ b) ⊗ c) ⊗ d = (a ⊗ b) ⊗ (c ⊗ d)

On the right, the two pairs can be computed independently. With more values, this grouping turns a long dependency chain into a balanced tree. Rearranging the values themselves would also require commutativity.

A set with an associative binary operation is a semigroup. Give it an identity, an element that leaves any value unchanged when combined with it, and it becomes a monoid. The identity also tells us what to return for an empty collection or a zeroth power.

Fast exponentiation reuses powers; segment trees store aggregates for intervals. A prefix scan needs the aggregate at every prefix of a sequence. Associativity supports each of these arrangements.12

Even a recurrence that appears sequential can sometimes use a scan. Take updates of the form x ↦ ax + b and represent each one by the pair (a, b). Applying (a, b) followed by (c, d) gives:

(a, b) ⋄ (c, d) = (ca, cb + d)For example: x ↦ 2x + 1, then x ↦ 3x + 4, becomes x ↦ 6x + 7.

Combine any number of these updates and the result still fits in a pair. That makes an associative scan useful. Arbitrary state transitions also compose, but storing or evaluating their composition may cost too much to help.2

Branching recurrences often need a second operation: one to combine alternatives, another to compose partial solutions. Semirings describe how those operations interact, giving us another way to organize dynamic programming when the recurrence has the right decomposition and the operations obey the required laws, including distributivity.10

Remove a state, keep its paths

A route from i to j can go directly or pass through k, possibly looping there before it leaves. Removing k means accounting for those possibilities in the edge that remains:

w′ij = wij ⊕ wik ⊗ (wkk)* ⊗ wkji and j survive the elimination; every term on the right uses the previous weights.

Read ⊕ as “combine alternatives,” ⊗ as “join successive steps,” and star as “repeat zero or more times.” Changing their meanings changes the question we can answer:

This is the connection between Floyd–Warshall, transitive closure, and converting an automaton to a regular expression. Repetition needs its own operation and laws; choosing two operators does not settle how loops behave. For finite shortest-path answers, the relevant routes must avoid negative cycles.

Matrix inversion fits the broader elimination framework through the equation X = I + AX. When the inverse exists, its solution is X = (I − A)−1. Here scalar closure means a* = 1/(1 − a), so some values are excluded. Unlike a total closed-semiring instance, arithmetic inversion needs valid pivots and the appropriate row and column updates. The inverse can exist even when the infinite sum of powers does not converge.3

Make progress without losing the answer

Euclid’s algorithm replaces a pair of numbers with the divisor and remainder:

(a, b) ↦ (b, r),   a = qb + rFor nonnegative integers and b > 0: 0 ≤ r < b.

The replacement preserves their common divisors. It also makes the second number smaller, and a nonnegative integer cannot keep shrinking forever. The same argument works for univariate polynomials over a field, with degree taking the place of size.1

Gaussian elimination also preserves the answer while canceling terms, but its coefficients need not shrink. Progress comes from moving through a finite number of pivot positions.

Gröbner-basis methods need two progress arguments. During polynomial reduction, each cancellation lowers the leading monomial in a well-founded order, an order with no infinite descending chain. Buchberger’s outer algorithm then adds nonzero remainders of S-polynomials to the basis. Each addition enlarges the ideal generated by leading terms, and the ascending-chain condition guarantees that this growth eventually stops.4

Find an easier representation

In coefficient form, polynomial multiplication requires convolution: each output coefficient collects products from several pairs of inputs. In point-value form, multiplication is immediate at each point:

(pq)(t) = p(t)q(t)

For p(x) = 1 + 2x and q(x) = 3 + 4x, the product has degree at most two. Three evaluations determine it:

Interpolation recovers 3 + 10x + 8x². At this size, converting back and forth is extra work. To gain speed, we need cheap conversions.

The discrete Fourier transform gives an exact relationship between cyclic convolution and pointwise multiplication:

F(a ∗ b) = F(a) ⊙ F(b)∗ is cyclic convolution; ⊙ is pointwise multiplication.

The FFT computes that transform efficiently. Over the complex numbers, suitable transform lengths let us convert in either direction in O(n log n) arithmetic operations. To multiply ordinary polynomials, we first add enough zero padding to prevent coefficients from wrapping around.56

Transform-based integer multiplication uses digit sequences in much the same way, then recovers exact coefficients and handles the carries.7 The transformation must preserve the operation and let us recover the answer. It only pays off if the whole computation, including conversion, costs less. Being a ring or group does not guarantee that such a transform exists.

Where greedy choice and iteration fit

Greedy selection gives us a precise test of which assumptions matter. Start with a finite set and a nonempty family of feasible subsets, closed under taking subsets. Give each element a nonnegative weight. Repeatedly add the heaviest element that keeps the selection feasible.

This rule finds a maximum-weight feasible set for every such weight assignment if and only if the family is a matroid. The matroid’s exchange axiom lets us justify the greedy choices.8 Success on one instance is not enough: the theorem covers every nonnegative weight assignment, with this selection rule and an additive objective.

Fixpoint iteration is another family. Apply a monotone map repeatedly, starting at the bottom of a finite-height lattice, and the sequence stabilizes at the least fixpoint. Many dataflow analyses use this structure. On an infinite domain, we may need widening or another convergence argument; a lattice alone does not make iteration terminate.9

One decomposition, several questions

A review of my private research suggested a practical use for this approach. A difference map partitions inputs into cells where two programs either agree everywhere or differ everywhere. The existing implementation can find the first differing input or count all of them. It can also answer the simpler question of whether a difference exists.11

The review explored a generic fold over those cells: summarize each one, then combine the summaries. Changing those operations added four queries in the scratch experiments: the last differing input, counts per output, the largest output gap, and the total output gap.

Q(C₁ ∪ ··· ∪ Cₘ) = q(C₁) ⊕ ··· ⊕ q(Cₘ)q must correctly summarize a whole cell; counts and totals require disjointness or explicit correction for overlap.

Associativity lets the fold regroup its summaries. With commutativity, it can also visit cells in any order. An idempotent operation satisfies a ⊕ a = a, so repeating a contribution does no harm; OR and minimum behave this way, as does maximum. Counting an input twice usually changes the answer.

The shortest-path experiment suggests routing constraints of the form x − y ≤ c to a suitable graph solver. This specialization is outside the inspected revision. Before its verdict can be used in a proof, it must produce the certificate expected by the checker.

One part of the implementation uses Fourier–Motzkin elimination with integer tightening and branching; another sums variables out symbolically. Both fit a broad elimination pattern. Relating them to Euclid’s algorithm would require a more specific argument.

Witness sets illustrate a restricted kind of representation change. For candidates with the same comparison cuts, affine-spanning witness points determine the affine outputs on each cell, subject to the stated failure conditions. An affine output is a linear expression plus a constant; arbitrary sample points may leave it underdetermined. This determining representation still falls short of a ring isomorphism or FFT-style diagonalization.11

When addition stops being associative

Consider addition that stops at a limit. On the integers from 0 to N, define a ⊕ b = min(N, a + b). Either grouping of three inputs gives min(N, a + b + c). This saturating addition is associative and commutative, with identity 0.

Now include negative inputs and clamp the result at both −N and N. Commutativity and the identity survive, but associativity fails. With N = 10:

(−10 ⊕ −10) ⊕ 1 = −9
−10 ⊕ (−10 ⊕ 1) = −10

In the first grouping, adding −10 to −10 hits the lower limit. Adding 1 then moves the result up to −9. In the second, −10 + 1 becomes −9 before the outer addition clamps the result back to −10. The parentheses determine when information is lost.

At N = 10⁹, the same example gives −999,999,999 and −1,000,000,000. Both cases were independently recomputed for this essay. The operator’s type signature gives no hint of the problem. A fold using it cannot be freely regrouped.

A contract could declare that it needs associativity and ask a checker to establish it. A counterexample would reject the proposed instance; a completed sound decision procedure could establish the bounded claim. Running out of budget would leave it unresolved. This contract feature is still a proposal.

What a synthesis system should remember

This is how I would organize a synthesis library. Store each procedure with the assumptions that justify it and its correctness argument. Record operation costs too. A label such as “graph algorithm” tells a search less about reuse than the laws the procedure needs.

A search could try to turn a recurrence into composition of affine maps, or recognize a path problem inside a set of constraints.

That connection has something to prove. Write E for the input encoding and D for the output decoder. If K is the reusable procedure, then for every valid input x:

D(K(E(x))) = Spec(x)For a functional specification, with encoding and decoding defined on the required domains.

Encoding must put the problem inside K’s domain of correctness, and decoding must recover the requested answer. A reversible map alone proves neither. Conversion costs also belong in any performance comparison.

A discovery-pattern library could retain the successful connection between a representation and a procedure, together with the conditions under which it was checked. A later search would have something to test and reuse. Automatic transfer between these domains remains a design goal; the experiments above have not demonstrated it.

The laws must hold for the implementation’s actual arithmetic, including its rounding and overflow rules. Failure behavior matters too. For saturating addition, changing the input range destroys associativity. A synthesis library needs to remember that range restriction along with the procedure.
