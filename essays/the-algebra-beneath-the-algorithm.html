The Algebra Beneath the Algorithm

How algebraic laws turn familiar algorithms into reusable methods.

Dana Edwards · November 12, 2026 · Research essay

Explore sections

Once you strip away concrete data types and examine the laws an algorithm uses, an algorithms textbook starts to look much smaller. Problems filed under arithmetic, graphs, strings, and signal processing repeatedly depend on the same few structures. A new domain can sometimes be reached by changing the values and operations beneath an existing procedure.

What must remain true for this procedure to work?

Alexander Stepanov and Paul McJones organize Elements of Programming around the relationship between algorithms and the mathematical requirements that justify them. Those requirements determine where an algorithm applies.1

Several families make this way of thinking concrete. They overlap, and they connect algorithms at different levels: sometimes through the same procedure, sometimes through a shared law or proof pattern. Four will serve as a starting point; greedy selection and fixpoint iteration will show why the list should remain open.

Regrouping without changing the answer

Suppose we combine four values. Associativity lets us change the parentheses while preserving their order:

((a ⊗ b) ⊗ c) ⊗ d = (a ⊗ b) ⊗ (c ⊗ d)

The two pairs on the right can be computed independently. With more values, balanced grouping reduces the depth of the dependency chain. Associativity permits regrouping; commutativity would additionally permit reordering.

A set with an associative binary operation is a semigroup. Add an identity element—something that changes nothing when combined with a value—and it becomes a monoid. The identity supplies the result for an empty collection or a zeroth power.

Fast exponentiation reuses powers of the same value. A prefix scan produces the aggregate at every prefix. A segment tree stores aggregates for intervals. Their control flow and outputs differ, but associativity licenses all three forms of regrouping.12

Some recurrences become scans after a change of representation. Consider updates of the form x ↦ ax + b. Store each update as a pair (a, b). Applying (a, b) and then (c, d) gives:

(a, b) ⋄ (c, d) = (ca, cb + d)For example: x ↦ 2x + 1, then x ↦ 3x + 4, becomes x ↦ 6x + 7.

Composition is associative, and these pairs give a compact representation of it. That makes a scan useful. Merely observing that arbitrary state transitions compose does not make every dynamic program an efficient parallel scan: the composed transition may be expensive to represent or evaluate.2

Dynamic programming crosses these boundaries. A chain can expose associative composition; branching recurrences often need both combination of alternatives and composition of partial solutions. Semirings provide a powerful framework for that second case when the required distributive laws and decomposition conditions hold. “All dynamic programming requires a semiring” would be a stronger claim.10

Eliminating an intermediate state

A route can go directly from i to j, or visit k, loop there, and leave. Eliminating k folds those possibilities into one edge:

w′ij = wij ⊕ wik ⊗ (wkk)* ⊗ wkji and j survive the elimination; every term on the right uses the previous weights.

Here ⊕ combines alternatives, ⊗ joins successive steps, and star accounts for zero or more repetitions.

This connects Floyd–Warshall, transitive closure, and automaton-to-regex conversion. The closure operation and its laws matter; two operators alone are insufficient. Finite shortest-path answers also require the relevant routes to avoid negative cycles.

Matrix inversion fits the broader elimination framework through X = I + AX, giving X = (I − A)−1 when the inverse exists. Ordinary arithmetic needs defined pivots, with scalar closure a* = 1/(1 − a); it is not a total closed-semiring instance. Pivot handling and row/column updates matter. The inverse need not equal a convergent infinite sum of powers.3

Preserving the answer while making progress

Euclid’s algorithm repeatedly replaces a pair by a divisor and remainder:

(a, b) ↦ (b, r),   a = qb + rFor nonnegative integers and b > 0: 0 ≤ r < b.

Two facts do the work. The common divisors are preserved, and the remainders cannot decrease forever. Replace integers with univariate polynomials over a field, and degree supplies the decreasing measure.1

Gaussian elimination and Gröbner-basis methods share a broader pattern: cancel terms while preserving the relevant solution set or algebraic relation. Their progress arguments differ. Gaussian elimination advances through finitely many pivot positions; its coefficients need not shrink.

In polynomial reduction, a well-founded monomial order prevents an endless descent of leading monomials. Buchberger’s outer algorithm adds nonzero remainders of S-polynomials to the basis. Its termination relies on the strictly growing ideals generated by leading terms eventually having to stop growing.4

Calling all three “the same division loop” would discard the distinction that proves termination. The reusable questions are more valuable: what does each step preserve, what counts as progress, and why can progress not continue forever?

Changing coordinates to simplify an operation

Multiply two polynomials in coefficient form and their coefficients interact through convolution. Evaluate them at suitable points, and multiplication becomes pointwise:

(pq)(t) = p(t)q(t)

For p(x) = 1 + 2x and q(x) = 3 + 4x, three evaluations determine their degree-at-most-two product:

Interpolation recovers 3 + 10x + 8x². This tiny example illustrates the representation change; it makes no claim of a speedup.

The discrete Fourier transform makes the corresponding relationship exact for cyclic convolution:

F(a ∗ b) = F(a) ⊙ F(b)∗ is cyclic convolution; ⊙ is pointwise multiplication.

The FFT is a fast algorithm for computing that transform. Over the complex numbers, suitable transform lengths allow the forward and inverse conversions in O(n log n) arithmetic operations. Ordinary polynomial multiplication uses enough zero padding to prevent wraparound.56

Transform-based integer multiplication applies this idea to digit sequences, with additional work for exact coefficient recovery and carries.7 The broader principle is conditional: find a reversible representation that preserves the operation and makes the whole computation cheaper. Being a ring or group does not itself supply such a transform.

Two more structures worth recognizing

Greedy selection has a particularly sharp boundary. Start with a finite set and a nonempty family of feasible subsets closed under taking subsets. For every assignment of nonnegative element weights, selecting the heaviest remaining feasible element gives a maximum-weight feasible set if and only if the family is a matroid. Its exchange axiom supplies the crucial additional structure.8

The quantifiers matter. A greedy method succeeding on one weighted instance does not establish the matroid property, and the theorem concerns this particular selection rule and additive objective.

Fixpoint iteration supplies another family. For a monotone map on a finite-height lattice, iteration from the bottom stabilizes at the least fixpoint. This underlies many dataflow analyses. Infinite domains may need widening or additional convergence arguments; having a lattice alone does not ensure that ordinary iteration stops.9

The lesson survives the longer list: identify the law that licenses a procedure, then look for other places where it holds.

What this lens revealed in Crucible

A review of my Crucible research suggested a practical use for this perspective: separate a checked decomposition of a problem from the question asked of that decomposition. A difference map partitions the inputs into cells where two programs agree everywhere or differ everywhere. The existing implementation asks whether a difference exists, where the first one occurs, and how many inputs differ.11

The review then explored a generic fold over these cells. Changing the aggregation operation and the way a cell is summarized reportedly added the last differing input, counts per output, the largest output gap, and the total output gap.

Q(C₁ ∪ ··· ∪ Cₘ) = q(C₁) ⊕ ··· ⊕ q(Cₘ)q must correctly summarize a whole cell; counts and totals require disjointness or explicit correction for overlap.

Associativity permits regrouping. Commutativity permits visiting cells in any order. Idempotence—a ⊕ a = a—makes repeated contributions harmless for operations such as OR, minimum, and maximum. For counting, the same input appearing twice usually changes the answer. These are separate permissions, each justified by a separate law.

The shortest-path result suggests recognizing constraints of the form x − y ≤ c and selecting a specialized solver. That is a useful application of the algebraic view, but it does not establish that closed-semiring elimination was already present in the committed code. A faster verdict also needs the certificate expected by the consuming proof checker.

The other connections need equally precise names. The inspected linear.py uses Fourier–Motzkin elimination with integer tightening and branching; count.py sums variables out symbolically. These fit a broad elimination pattern, without becoming instances of Euclid’s remainder algorithm.

The witness-set representation has an exact, narrower interpretation. For candidates restricted to the same comparison cuts, affine-spanning witness points determine the affine outputs on each cell, subject to the stated failure conditions. Agreement on arbitrary sample points would not suffice. This is a determining representation for a restricted function class; a ring isomorphism or FFT-style diagonalization would require additional structure.11

A familiar operator can fail the contract

The most revealing example from the review is saturating addition. On the integers from 0 to N, define a ⊕ b = min(N, a + b). Both bracketings of three inputs equal min(N, a + b + c). The operation is associative and commutative, and 0 is its identity.

Now allow negative inputs and clamp at both −N and N. Commutativity and the identity survive. Associativity fails. With N = 10:

(−10 ⊕ −10) ⊕ 1 = −9
−10 ⊕ (−10 ⊕ 1) = −10

The same counterexample scales to N = 10⁹, producing −999,999,999 and −1,000,000,000. It was independently recomputed for this essay. A fold using this operation cannot be freely rebracketed, even though its name, type signature, commutativity, and identity all look suitable.

A contract could therefore declare the algebra its algorithm requires. A checker could search for a violation of associativity, for example. A counterexample rejects the proposed instance. A completed sound decision procedure can establish the bounded claim; a budget stop cannot. This is a proposed ZAL/Crucible contract feature, not a claim that these declarations and checks are already integrated.

The proof is part of the reusable component

These connections change how I would organize a synthesis system. A library entry should contain a procedure, its assumptions, its correctness argument, and the cost of the operations it calls. Retrieving an algorithm by domain label alone leaves much of that knowledge inaccessible.

For a new problem, the search can ask which representation exposes the required laws. A numerical recurrence might become composition of affine maps. A route problem might become elimination over a suitable algebra. A convolution might become pointwise multiplication after a transform.

That introduces a concrete proof obligation. If E encodes inputs, K is the reusable procedure, and D decodes the result, then for every valid input x:

D(K(E(x))) = Spec(x)For a functional specification, with encoding and decoding defined on the required domains.

The system must establish that the encoded inputs satisfy K’s assumptions and that decoding recovers the requested answer. A reversible encoding by itself proves neither claim. The conversion costs must also be included before claiming an improvement.

This suggests a discovery-pattern library: retain the successful representation, the algorithm it enabled, and the conditions under which the connection was checked. The next search can begin with that relationship. This is a design proposal for organizing synthesis, not evidence that an implementation already performs these transfers automatically.

Reusing a proof requires checking the laws of the actual operations, including rounding, overflow, and failure behavior. The relevant carrier is the declared domain of the implementation, with its exact semantics. Algebraic names do not discharge those obligations.

A reusable algorithm is a procedure together with the conditions under which it deserves to be reused.
