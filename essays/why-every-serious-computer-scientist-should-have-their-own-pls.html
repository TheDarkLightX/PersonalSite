Why Every Serious Computer Scientist Should Have Their Own PLs

Dana Edwards · November 19, 2026

“Who the heck cares if anyone uses programming languages?” Kiran Gopinathan asks. She points out that most languages have just one user, the person who built them. For her, that’s a reason to stop worrying about adoption and enjoy the research. I care about that one user. When I designed ZAL, I had a particular person’s way of thinking in mind. It happened to be mine.

I regard computer science as a branch of mathematics. Programming is a means to an end. If I’m stuck on a problem, I can try harder to find a program that solves it, or I can change the language I’m searching in. My favorite example is a search that can get the right score while missing half the winning answers.

Four ways to score three

In a paper on RNA folding, Maik Riechert, Christian Höner zu Siederdissen, and Peter F. Stadler work through a strand just six letters long, AGCGUU. Their example awards one point for each pair of bases in a fold. Three points is the most six bases can earn. Four different folds reach that score.

You can see the difference with a pencil. Write the six letters in a row and number them. In one winning fold, draw arcs above the letters joining 1 to 6, 2 to 5, and 3 to 4. They nest neatly. In another, join 1 to 5, 2 to 6, and 3 to 4. Now the first two arcs cross. That crossing is the feature RNA researchers call a pseudoknot.

A grammar that allows only nested or disjoint pairs can find the first fold. It can even report the correct maximum of three. But two of the four winning folds contain crossings, so it will miss both however long I let it run. The authors use a multiple context-free grammar that can build separated pieces of a fold together. Its rules admit those crossings. The scoring rule can stay as simple as one point per pair. If I tested my program only against the number three, I’d never notice which answers my language had ruled out. Riechert and colleagues, Appendix A · Worked folds, p. 34 (PDF)

In my one-operation calculator, even the ability to name an intermediate result changes what a search can find. The same eight-round recipe takes 781,249 instructions when I copy out every step and 33 when I can reuse a saved answer. Give a synthesizer a budget of 40 instructions. The language I want to read

When I think about an auction, I picture the bids and ask who gets to choose the price. A loop over an order book comes later. In ZAL’s controlled English, I can give the caller the choice of any price from 10 to 12, or I can give that choice to the exchange. Both rules allow a trade at 10. Suppose I ask for 12 and the exchange insists on 10. Under the first rule, I have a complaint. Under the second, I gave the exchange that discretion. That’s the distinction I want to see when I read the rule. Saying “any” on purpose

The Racket designers have long argued for building a language around the problem at hand. LLMs change what it costs me to try. I can ask a model to draft two interpreters and spend the afternoon on the one auction where their answers ought to differ. That’s what I mean by an intellectual golden age for computer scientists. I can afford to find out I was wrong about my first design.

There’s a reasonable bet in the other direction. An AI writing for another AI might prefer a dense encoding. An engineering team might keep a familiar language for its libraries alone. My prediction concerns the high-level languages humans still have to read, review, and think in. When two languages can do the same job, I expect people to keep the one in which they can spot a costly mistake, or whose rules let a checker catch it for them. Over time, that pressure favors safer, more readable languages.

I expect personal programming languages to become an ordinary part of a computer scientist’s working life. And I mean languages, plural. Controlled English suits me when I’m assigning a permission. When I’m working out which states are reachable, I’d sooner have the diagram. ZAL gives me room for both. Your language might look quite different. I’d like to see it.
