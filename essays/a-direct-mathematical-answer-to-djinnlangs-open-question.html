A Direct Mathematical Answer to Djinnlang’s Open Question

Dana Edwards · November 19, 2026

Why should a programmer have to fix the wording of an error message? In Djinnlang, an LLM can rewrite a function, but every correct implementation must give the same output for the same input. Simon Henniger, Stephen Chong, and Nada Amin ask how to relax that rule when several answers would do. Djinnlang, §§2 and 10

In Saying “any” on purpose, I make their example executable. A bank must refuse an over-limit withdrawal and leave the account unchanged. Each time it refuses, the system may choose either approved wording. ZAL’s controlled English fixes who chooses and how long the choice lasts. There is one way to read the withdrawal rule and two ways to obey it. Saying “any” on purpose, §§4–5

What the wording gives away

Write A(x) for the permitted outputs on input x. A policy picks one, f(x)∈A(x). If every outcome in A(x) leaves the balance unchanged, so does f(x). That proof takes one substitution.

Privacy takes a different proof. Suppose the account holds a secret bit. Keep the public inputs fixed and number the messages 1 and 2. Either message is legal for either bit, so A(0)=A(1)={1,2}. Now pick message 1 when the bit is 0 and message 2 when it is 1. Every choice is legal. But the customer can read the bit straight off the screen. Under this policy the possible messages are {1} for one secret and {2} for the other. Their inequality is the counterexample. Across five finite models, only 12 of the 80 deterministic policies examined preserved the studied two-run law. Saying “any” on purpose, §§6.7–6.8

When the scheduler chooses

Djinnlang also asks about freedom in the order of execution. Assume each enabled move has one outcome and every step lowers a natural-number rank. My companion paper proves that all complete schedules from a fixed start agree on the output sequence and final state exactly when both arms of every reachable fork can rejoin at one state having emitted the same sequence since the fork. Local commutation and its limits, §5

Take any fork and finish both arms. Decreasing rank guarantees they can finish. If all schedules agree, their endpoints coincide. Strike out the common output before the fork and the remaining sequences still match.

For the converse, use induction on rank. At a terminal state the remaining output is empty. At a fork, each successor has lower rank and already has a unique completed result. Follow both arms to their join and append the same completion. This produces matching results on the two arms, and uniqueness rules out any other result on either arm. Lean checked both directions. For a service that can run forever, whether a waiting customer ever gets a reply remains a separate question.
